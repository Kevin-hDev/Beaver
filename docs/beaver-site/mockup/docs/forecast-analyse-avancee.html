Four calculations on your data

At the end of every forecast, Beaver produces a decomposition, an anomaly detection, a drift measurement and a variable importance. This page says where those figures come from, what exactly they describe, and what they don't say.

What it is, and when it is computed

It has no button. The advanced analysis is produced automatically at the end of every forecast. You have nothing to launch, and you can't avoid it. It is saved in the analysis file, so it is kept with the forecast and available in the exports.

It costs no model call. The four calculations are done on your machine, from data already in memory. A remote forecast returns none of it: the calculation is done afterwards, locally.

Note No button: the analysis is done at every forecast, computed locally even when the forecast comes from a remote model, and saved with the analysis.

It speaks about your history, not your forecast

This is the most important point on the page, and it's counter-intuitive. The decomposition and the drift are computed on the data you supplied, not on the predicted values. The anomalies follow from that, since they concern the decomposition's residuals. Variable importance is computed on the source file's rows.

In other words: these four blocks describe the past. A flagged anomaly is an odd point in your history, not a surprising forecast. A detected drift says that the end of your history no longer resembles its beginning — it's a warning about the forecast's reliability, not a measurement of the forecast itself.

Conversely, the Trend, Uncertainty and Highlights blocks in the same section are computed from the predicted values. Two origins, one screen.

Key point Decomposition, anomalies and drift all concern the history you supplied. A flagged anomaly is an odd point in the past, not a surprising forecast.

The four advanced-analysis blocks are therefore all collapsed on opening, and the three open ones are those computed from the forecast. If a block looks empty, expand it.

A series selector appears at the top as soon as there is more than one series. Decomposition, anomalies and drift are computed series by series; variable importance, on the other hand, is computed across all series combined.

For the record, the three blocks computed from the forecast: Trend gives the direction — Rising, Falling or Stable, with a ±2% threshold — the total change in value and in percentage, the start value and the end value. Uncertainty gives the average range, the maximum range and the period where it is at its maximum, with a warning if the maximum range exceeds 1.5 times the average. Highlights gives four events: the highest point, the lowest, the strongest rise and the strongest drop.

Decomposition

Separating a series into three layers: a trend, a repeating pattern, and what's left.

The two decomposition methods, chosen automatically
Method shownChosen whenWhat it does
Classical additiveThe frequency implies a period greater than 1 and the history covers at least two complete periodsMoving average over one period, then average seasonal effect per phase, recentred
Moving averageEvery other caseMoving average over 5 points, no seasonality extracted

The period is derived from the frequency, exactly as for the backtest baselines: day 7, hour 24, month 12, quarter 4, week 52, second and minute 60, business day 5, year 1. No manual setting. Below 5 points, the decomposition isn't done at all and the block shows “Insufficient history for decomposition.”

Three values are shown: the Method, the Seasonal period — or “Not detected” when it equals 1 — and the Seasonal strength as a percentage. The last one reads as “the share of what isn't the trend that is explained by the seasonal pattern”. It is only computed by the additive method; with the moving average, the block shows “Unavailable”.

The decomposed points themselves — observed, trend, seasonal, residual, date by date — are saved in the analysis file but aren't displayed anywhere: the screen only shows the three summary values. They do come out in the exports, however.

Residual anomalies

Beaver doesn't look for “high” or “low” values: it looks for the points that depart from what the decomposition expected, that is, the large residuals.

The method is robust, which has a practical consequence: a few extreme values don't distort the detection of the others. The centre is the median of the residuals, the spread their median absolute deviation.

The calculation is done per seasonal phase when the phase has at least 4 points and its residuals aren't all identical; otherwise it falls back to the whole series. Concretely, with a daily frequency, Mondays are compared with Mondays.

The threshold is 3.5. Below that, nothing is flagged. A point at 3.5 departs by three and a half times the usual spread of its phase. Two severity levels: high above 6, medium between 3.5 and 6.

A hundred anomalies at most are kept, the strongest first. The screen only shows eight per series, each in the form “Residual anomaly”, the observed value, then the date and the score. When there are none: “No residual anomaly detected.” The anomalies also appear on the main chart, as a toggleable layer named “Residual anomalies”.

Note An anomaly is judged against its season. With a daily frequency, a Monday is compared with the other Mondays, not with all the days — when the history contains enough of them.

Drift

Drift answers one question: does the end of your history still resemble its beginning? If not, a model trained on the whole thing starts from a past that is no longer current.

At least 24 points are required, otherwise the block shows “Insufficient history to measure drift.” Two windows of the same size are compared: the first points against the last. Their size is a third of the history, brought between 8 and 256 points.

The four gaps measured between the beginning and the end of the history
GapWhat it compares
Mean shiftThe difference of the means, relative to the common spread
Spread changeThe ratio of the variances, in absolute value
Slope changeThe difference of the slopes, brought to the window's scale
Distribution shiftThe distance between the two samples, from 0 to 1

The drift score is the largest of the four, the distribution shift counting double. There is therefore no averaging: a single indicator in the red is enough to trigger the alert. Two thresholds: drift detected from 1.0, high severity from 2.0.

Three values are shown: the Status — “Drift detected” as a warning, or “Stable” — the Drift score to two decimals, and the Distribution shift as a percentage. The other three gaps are computed and saved, but not shown: the visible score therefore doesn't say which of the four phenomena produced it.

Warning A detected drift doesn't make the forecast wrong. It says the end of your history no longer resembles its beginning. It's a reason to look at the backtest more closely, not a verdict.

Variable importance

This block only has content if your forecast uses context variables. Without them, the screen shows “Importance unavailable or insufficient data.”

What Beaver measures exactly. For each variable, it builds pairs: on one side the target's change from one step to the next, on the other the variable's value at that step. It fits a line between the two over the first 70 per cent of the pairs, chronologically, and keeps the remaining 30% to check.

Importance is then measured by permutation. Beaver compares this line's error on the check portion with the error obtained when the variable's values are shifted circularly — three different shifts, of which the average is taken. If shuffling the variable clearly degrades the prediction, it carried information; if it changes nothing, the score is zero. The circular shift, rather than a random shuffle, preserves the order of time.

Minimum required per variable: 12 training pairs and 6 check pairs. A variable that doesn't reach that is set aside without being flagged. If none passes, the block shows the unavailability message.

The scores are normalised so they sum to 100%, then sorted from strongest to weakest. The screen shows at most eight variables, each with its percentage and its direction — positive, negative or neutral according to the slope's sign.

The calculation's reliability is established but not shown. Beaver files it in three levels according to the number of check points — high from 30, moderate from 12, low below — and passes it to the agent, with the instruction to always mention it. The screen shows nothing of it: a percentage read on screen may rest on six points as easily as on three hundred.

Warning Variable importance doesn't say what the model did. It is computed by a small linear model internal to Beaver, independently of the forecast model chosen. It measures a link in your data — “this variable is related to the target's variations” — not a decision by the model.

What the advanced analysis doesn't say

  • It doesn't judge the model. None of these four calculations compares the forecast with reality: for that, you need a backtest — see Evaluation and comparison.
  • It doesn't detect anomalies in the future. The anomalies concern the history supplied.
  • A calculation that goes off the rails is erased silently. A final pass checks that every value is finite; otherwise the decomposition falls back to “insufficient data”, the anomalies concerned are removed and the drift reset to zero. An “insufficient” block can therefore flag a failed calculation as much as a history that is too short.
  • It isn't replayed when you change something without running a new forecast: it is produced once, at the end of the calculation, with a timestamp.

Where it turns up elsewhere

In the exports. The decomposition points, the anomalies, the drift and the variable importance come out in the workbook and in the PDF report — see Exports.

In the Report section of the Forecast workspace, which shows the same Analysis section next to the export menu — see Scenarios, notes and reports.

With the agent. When the agent consults an analysis, it receives the decomposition in summary form, the strongest anomalies, the variable importance and the drift, accompanied by a sentence reminding it of the exact nature of each figure. The number of anomalies passed along is capped, and an indicator flags the truncation. Its Forecast tools are off by default — see Forecast, on the tools side.
