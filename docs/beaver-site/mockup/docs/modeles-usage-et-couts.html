Tracking what you spend

Beaver measures every request sent to a provider and shows you what it amounts to. This page explains where those figures come from, why they do not always match your bill, and why no prices appear on this site.

What Beaver measures

Every request sent to a provider is measured: the model used, the tokens consumed on input and output, the duration, the outcome — completed, interrupted, cancelled, failed.

These measurements are recorded locally, in the application data. They are sent to no one. The usage screen updates live: as soon as a request finishes, the counters move.

Privacy Consumption measurements stay on your machine. No usage data is sent to Beaver or to a third party. See Privacy.

The three sources of figures

  • What Beaver counted itself. The local ledger, filled in request after request. It is complete for usage made from Beaver, and it knows nothing else.
  • What the provider announces. Some providers return, in their responses, the state of the account's limits: requests remaining, quota, renewal window. Beaver reads and displays them.
  • The request-by-request detail. A log of recent requests, with their model, their outcome and their consumption.

Note Beaver's figures and your provider's may differ, and both are right: Beaver only counts what went through it. If the same account is used elsewhere — another tool, a script, the provider's website — the provider's totals will be higher.

Exact cost and estimated cost

Exact cost — some providers return the request's real cost in their response. Beaver records it as is. That is a billed figure, not an estimate.

Estimated cost — otherwise, Beaver computes it: input tokens, output tokens, and tokens read from or written to the cache, each at its own rate. The calculation is fine-grained — it does not charge full price for tokens re-read from a cache — but it remains an estimate.

One guardrail is worth knowing about: if the calculation gives an aberrant result — infinite, negative, or above one million dollars — Beaver declines to show a cost rather than display an absurdity. A missing figure beats a wrong one.

Where the prices come from

Beaver hard-codes no price. It downloads a public price catalog, keeps it locally and refreshes it by re-downloading only if the source has changed. Three protections surround that download:

  • only the expected source is accepted;
  • the file size is capped;
  • a catalog with fewer than a hundred entries is rejected — a truncated or emptied source does not overwrite a valid catalog.

For you, that means prices stay up to date without you doing anything, and without Beaver needing an update. It is also the reason no price appears on this site: the authority is the provider, and Beaver follows a living source.

What is not priced

Three cases where no cost appears — the absence is not a defect:

  • Local models. They cost nothing in money. Their token consumption is measured, not their price.
  • Account sign-ins. Usage is covered by a subscription, not billed per request: showing a cost would make no sense. See Account sign-in.
  • A few recent models whose pricing cannot be expressed by the simple input-output calculation. Beaver prefers to show nothing rather than an approximate figure.

The request log

Every request leaves a trace: identifier, model, conversation turn, attempt, outcome, tokens.

That log is there to answer "what happened?" without having to reproduce the problem — a request may have failed, been retried, or been cancelled part-way, and the usage screen alone would not say so.

Usage is also attributed to its origin: an ordinary conversation, or a scheduled task. So if your consumption looks high, you can see whether it comes from what you did, or from what Beaver did on its own during the night.

Changing a key resets the remote counters

When a key or a connection changes, the information coming from the provider is erased. The reason: it described another account's state, and keeping it would amount to showing one account's quota for another.

The local ledger, on the other hand, is kept — it is the history of what Beaver did, independently of the account used.
