Knowing what your forecast is worth

A forecast on its own doesn't say whether it's any good. Beaver replays it in the past, over periods whose outcome it already knows, and compares what the model would have predicted with what actually happened. This page explains how that measurement is built and how to read it.

What evaluation does

The name shown is “Rolling temporal validation”. The essential point: during each trial, the “to be predicted” part is erased from the data before being sent to the model. The model therefore can't cheat, not even by accident. The interface says it in one line: “The backtest compares the current model with four baselines without training on future data.”

Evaluation is launched from the Evaluation section of the Forecast workspace, with the “Run backtest” button.

How the history is cut up

Beaver builds a backtest plan before launching anything.

The evaluation horizon is the smallest of three numbers: your forecast's horizon, 256 steps — an absolute ceiling — and a third of the number of points in the shortest series.

The number of windows is 3, bounded between 1 and 5. If the history doesn't allow three to be fitted, Beaver uses fewer. The number of windows isn't adjustable from the screen: the interface always asks for three.

The windows shift through time. Each window trains on everything that precedes it and tests on the following points; the last one ends on the most recent known points. The training window grows with each trial — that's what “rolling” means. The absolute minimum is 4 points per series; below that, the backtest is refused.

The four comparison baselines

An error figure on its own says nothing. Beaver therefore systematically evaluates four simple methods over exactly the same windows, and shows them next to the model.

The four baseline methods and what they do
Name shownWhat it doesMinimum points
NaiveRepeats the last known value1
Seasonal NaiveRepeats the last complete cycleA whole period
DriftExtends the straight line joining the first and last point2
ETSExponential smoothing with trend, best pair of coefficients among 0.2 / 0.5 / 0.83

The seasonal period is derived from the frequency, with no setting available: second 60, minute 60, hour 24, day 7, business day 5, week 52, month 12, quarter 4, year 1. Any unrecognised frequency falls back to 1, that is, no seasonality.

If none of the four baselines completes, the entire backtest fails. That's consistent: without a baseline, a ranking is meaningless.

The baselines also get a confidence range, built from their own past errors. All four methods are therefore judged on the same grid as the model, intervals included.

The table shows only four of them: Model, MASE, sMAPE, MAE, then Coverage, Duration and Result. RMSE, bias, stability and quantile loss are computed, saved in the analysis and used to break ties in the ranking, but aren't shown on screen — you find them in the exports. Values are rounded to two decimals; the duration is in milliseconds below one second, in seconds beyond.

A chart accompanies the table: one bar per evaluated model, in sMAPE, with a dotted mean line. Its title is “Backtest error (sMAPE)” and its vertical axis carries the label “sMAPE (%)”. The current model's bar, the baselines' bars and the other models' bars carry three distinct colors.

One measurement stands apart: memory. While evaluating a local model, Beaver samples the memory of the engine's entire process tree ten times a second and keeps the maximum. It is only measured for local models — a remote model consumes nothing on your side — it serves to break ties between two models of equal quality, and it isn't shown anywhere.

The most useful landmark MASE 1.00 is the “I repeat the last value” level. Below 1, the model does better than a naive forecast; above, it does worse — and a model that does worse than a repetition is of no use on that data.

Coverage: the confidence range's promise

Alongside the error metrics, Beaver checks a second thing: does the confidence range keep its promise? An 80% range should contain the true value roughly 80 times out of 100.

The calculation produces five figures: theoretical coverage — the level requested — measured coverage — the share of points that actually fell within the range — the range's mean width, the half-width of the residuals at the requested level, and the number of points used.

The table shows the first two, separated by a slash: “measured / theoretical”. Reading “62.5% / 80.0%” means: the range promised 80%, it only delivered 62.5% — it is too narrow.

The ranking, and “Beats the baseline”

Results are sorted by ten successive criteria, each only breaking ties left by the previous one: MASE, then quantile loss, then coverage gap, then sMAPE, then MAE, then RMSE, then absolute value of the bias, then stability, then maximum memory, then duration.

Two remarks about that order. It puts the quality of the confidence range in second position, ahead of every other error metric: Beaver considers that a model that is just as wrong but states its uncertainty better is the better one. And it puts speed dead last: speed never wins it for a less accurate model.

A model that failed is ranked after all the others. The rank shown is a plain number in the first column.

The Result column says three things: “Reference” for the four simple methods; “Beats the baseline” or “Does not beat the baseline” for a model — the comparison is against the best of the four, not against their average, and by the same ten criteria; or a failure reason when the evaluation didn't complete.

What the backtest changes in the forecast itself

This is the least visible and most useful effect: a backtest recalibrates your forecast's confidence range.

When the analysis's model has been evaluated with at least 3 points of measurement, Beaver takes the half-width of the observed residuals and widens the forecast's low and high bounds to at least that value. The bounds are never narrowed. In other words: if the model was wrong by more than its range announced, the displayed range widens to reflect the errors actually observed. The zero floor for quantities that can't be negative is then reapplied, and the ordering of the three bounds revalidated.

This change is saved: it modifies the stored analysis, not just the display.

Note The backtest widens your forecast's confidence range. If the model was wrong by more than its range announced, the range widens after the backtest to reflect its real errors. It is never narrowed.

When a model can't be evaluated

A model can be refused before any calculation, or fail along the way. The interface groups these cases into six messages.

The six failure messages and what they cover
Message shownReal causes
Insufficient historyNot enough points for the baseline concerned
Cloud service not configuredNo API key, or cloud forbidden in this context
Model not installedLocal model absent from the disk
Insufficient resourcesThe machine doesn't have the memory the model asks for
Incomplete evaluationThe engine didn't start, a window failed, or the predictions returned are incomplete
Result unavailableEverything else

The checks made before launching a model: it exists and can predict; it accepts the analysis's confidence level; if your data contains several series, it must be able to handle them; if you use context variables, it must accept the future context; a local model must be installed and the machine must have the resources; a remote model requires an API key, and cloud permission if the model evaluated isn't the analysis's own.

Warning Evaluating a remote model sends your data as many times as there are windows — three requests by default, in addition to the original forecast. See TimeGPT (cloud).

The Comparison section and the weighted ensemble

Evaluation and Comparison are the same screen, with only two differences: the button — “Run backtest” on one side, creating an ensemble on the other, which requires at least two successfully evaluated models — and the text shown when nothing has been measured yet: “Run a backtest in Evaluation to obtain a reliable ranking.”

Two paths lead to the backtest, and they don't offer the same possibilities.

From the screen, the button always runs the backtest on the analysis's model, with three windows: a single model against the four baselines. And since each backtest replaces the previous one, you can't accumulate two models by running it twice.

From the conversation, it's different. The forecast_backtest tool accepts up to five models and a number of windows from 1 to 5, and forecast_analyze builds the ensemble from two to four successfully evaluated models. The agent also has forecast_compare_models to re-read the saved ranking. These seven tools are off by default and are turned on in the agent's settings — see Forecast, on the tools side.

Key point Comparing several models and the weighted ensemble go through the conversation today, not through the Forecast workspace's buttons. A backtest launched with the mouse measures a single model against the four baselines.

The weighted ensemble combines several already-evaluated models into a single curve:

  • you need at least 2 models evaluated without failure, and at most 4;
  • each model's weight is the inverse of its MASE, normalised so the sum is 1: a model twice as accurate weighs twice as much;
  • the models that aren't the analysis's own are re-run on the same data to produce their forecast;
  • the forecasts must be perfectly aligned — same dates, same series, same number of points — otherwise the creation is refused;
  • predictions and confidence bounds are averaged with those weights, and the result is refused if it produces a badly ordered interval.

On the main chart, the ensemble's curve carries the label “Weighted ensemble”.

Warning An ensemble is not a validated model. Each of its members was measured separately; their blend, on the other hand, was measured by nothing. Beaver writes this in the analysis file and shows it on screen: “Ensemble created from backtested models. The combination still requires separate validation.”
Warning Running a backtest again deletes the existing ensemble. The weights are computed from the current evaluation; when that one is replaced, so is the ensemble. It has to be recreated.
